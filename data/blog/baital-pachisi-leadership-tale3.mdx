# Leadership Insights from Baital Pachisi: Ancient Allegory vs. Modern Corporate Reality

In the 3rd tale of the _Baital Pachisi_, a loyal retainer named Virvar earns an exorbitant wage from King Rupsen and repays that debt by sacrificing his own lineage to avert a fatal omen over the kingdom. Overcome with grief and moral shock, King Rupsen draws his sword to take his own life at the altar rather than accept a crown saved at such a cost.

When asked whose sacrifice was greater, King Vikramaditya delivers the verdict: **The King's.**

Virvar was an employee bound by duty and pay; the King owed no servitude to Virvar, yet chose to surrender his power and life out of pure conscience.

Folktales speak in mythic extremes. In today's corporate world, how much of this ancient verdict holds up, and where does it fail?

---

### What to Keep: What Leaders Should Still Do Today

#### 1. Moral Parity and Public Shielding

- **The Story:** The leader refused to let a subordinate bear the existential cost while sitting comfortably on the throne.
- **Modern Practice:** Extreme ownership. When an outage happens or a product flops, weak executives point fingers downward. Exceptional leaders stand in front of the board, clients, and press to say: _"The strategy and resource allocation were mine; the accountability stops with me."_

#### 2. Pay Top-of-Market, but Audit Character

- **The Story:** Rupsen paid 500 gold coins per day, but quietly verified Virvar's integrity, charity, and discipline before trusting him blindly.
- **Modern Practice:** Premium compensation attracts elite talent, but competence without ethics creates organizational liability. Leaders must evaluate alignment and values, not just raw output.

#### 3. Eradicate Asymmetric Risk

- **The Story:** The ruler recognized that expecting subordinates to absorb fatal downside while leadership captures all upside is morally bankrupt.
- **Modern Practice:** In an era of record executive bonuses paired with sudden mass layoffs, teams notice the gap immediately. Leaders must have visible skin in the game during downturns before demanding sacrifices from their workforce.

---

### What to Discard: What Leaders Must Not Do Today

#### 1. The Martyrdom Trap (Literal Self-Sacrifice)

- **The Story:** The King prepared to end his own life for an individual employee.
- **Why It Fails Now:** A CEO is not the company. Leaders carry a fiduciary and ethical obligation to the entire system, including customers, shareholders, suppliers, and hundreds of other workers. A CEO who falls on their sword over an isolated personnel crisis destabilizes the whole organization.

#### 2. Feudal Oaths and "Family" Rhetoric

- **The Story:** A retainer willingly destroyed his personal life and lineage for his employer.
- **Why It Fails Now:** Modern employment is an exchange of skilled labor for compensation and equity, not a lifetime blood pact. Demanding extreme, unsustainable sacrifices under the guise of "loyalty" or "we are a family" is simply exploitation in disguise.

---

### The Takeaway

Modern CEOs do not need to wield swords or practice martyrdom. But they must honor the central principle of Vikramaditya's judgment:

> **Authority is illegitimate when risk is entirely offloaded to those with the least power.**

If you expect dedication and accountability from your team, you must be prepared to absorb the hardest blows when things go south.
